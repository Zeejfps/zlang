============================================================================
rationale.txt  --  why the rules in spec.txt are the rules they are
============================================================================

THE ARGUMENTS, AND NOTHING ELSE. No rule is stated here. spec.txt says what
the syntax means; this file says why, and records what was weighed and
rejected to get there. It is the companion to the normative document, in
the way the C Rationale is the companion to the C standard: nothing here
constrains an implementation, and nothing here may be cited to decide what
a program means.

Every entry names the spec rule IDs it argues for, and the lines of the
original design document it was drawn from. The prose is the author's; the
editing is confined to making an argument readable by someone who has not
just read the rule it sat under. Where a pronoun's referent was in the
surrounding rule text, the referent is now spelled out. Where the original
said "the rule above", the rule's ID is now written.

Read glossary.txt, then spec.txt, then this file.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
ON THE NOTE NUMBERING, AND THE MISSING NOTE 5

The original design document contains NOTE 1, NOTE 2, NOTE 3, NOTE 4 and
NOTE 6. THERE IS NO NOTE 5. It was not misplaced in this extraction and it
is not elsewhere in the source: nothing in rules.txt, glossary.txt,
unified.z, json.z or ecs.z references a NOTE 5, and nothing explains the
gap. (unified.z:10 advertises "NOTE 1-9", which is stale in the other
direction -- there were never nine.)

DECISION: THE GAP IS KEPT. The notes are referred to here as N1, N2, N3,
N4 and N6, mapping one-to-one onto NOTE 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6. There is no N5
and there will not be one.

The reason is that the note numbers are cited from outside the document
and renumbering would silently break those citations. glossary.txt:200
says "See NOTE 6 in rules.txt"; unified.z:89 and :145 say "See NOTE 4",
unified.z:150 says "See NOTE 2"; ecs.z:226 and :836 cite "NOTE 3's rule";
ecs.z:1032 cites NOTE 4; json.z:747 cites NOTE 2. A gap a reader notices
once and finds explained here is cheaper than five stale pointers in three
corpus files, each of which would then have to be found and corrected.

RULES TO PROMOTE INTO spec.txt
============================================================================

FOUR PARAGRAPHS BELOW WERE CLASSED AS ARGUMENT AND ARE NOT. Each states a
rule that is stated nowhere else in the original, so none of them may be
annexed into this file. They are reproduced here for the spec author to
lift, and they should then be DELETED from this section rather than left
in two places.

  1. THE SHAPE / POLICY TEST.  rules.txt:651-654. The only statement of the
     test, and three normative rules derive from it -- abs.shape.1,
     abs.policy.1 and the operator split at op.members.1 /
     op.notoperators.1. Text:

         The test is not stylistic: CAN THERE LEGITIMATELY BE TWO OF THESE
         FOR ONE TYPE? Two orderings of u8, yes -> policy. Two ways for an
         Arena to allocate, no -> shape.

  2. ANYTHING THAT MUST AGREE BELONGS IN ONE BLOCK.  rules.txt:665-670.
     Stated nowhere in the rule sections, and corpus-supported: `Key<K>`
     carries hash and equals together at json.z:95-98. Text:

         ANYTHING THAT MUST AGREE BELONGS IN ONE BLOCK. A hash and an
         equals passed as two independent parameters can be supplied
         inconsistently -- a case-folding hash with a case-sensitive equals
         is a silently broken map that no type error catches. `Key<K>`
         carries both. Grouping is free here, because a policy is just a
         block.

  3. A SHAPE CONTAINS HOLES AND NOTHING ELSE.  rules.txt:696, the title of
     NOTE 3. The rule exists only as that title; the note's body argues for
     it and never restates it. Corpus-supported by every shape declared:
     Allocator, Writer, Ord, Formattable, Key, AnyStore, System, Fn, Fn2.
     Text:

         A shape contains holes and nothing else.

  4. A MEMBER HAS THE BLOCK'S COMPILE-TIME ARGS AND FIELDS IN SCOPE, AND
     MEMBERS ARE THE ONLY THING SHAPE MATCHING LOOKS AT.  rules.txt:727-728,
     the opening of NOTE 4. Stated only inside a note, and load-bearing:
     it is what lets `dump.pad` read a bare `indent` (ecs.z:733-735) and
     `List`'s field initialisers read a bare `allocator` and `capacity`
     (unified.z:164). Text:

         Methods in the block get T, M and the fields in scope for free,
         and they are the only thing shape matching looks at.

============================================================================
DECLARATION AND PROJECTION
============================================================================

R-01  The binder is the structural / nominal difference made visible
      Argues for:  bind.which.self.1, bind.which.named.1
      Source:      rules.txt:71-77

      Three spellings look like one act, which is why the projection's
      effect on them is worth stating: `Heap` writes `alloc :: ...` and
      `hex_u8` writes `format = ...` for what looks like the same act.
      Heap says `-> self` and satisfies Allocator STRUCTURALLY -- it never
      names it, so there is no hole in scope and `alloc` is created. A
      policy instance NAMES its type in the projection --
      `hex_u8 :: -> Formattable<u8>` -- so the holes are in scope and it
      fills one. The binder is the structural / nominal difference made
      visible.

      Alternatives weighed: none recorded. The alternative not taken is
      implicit: a separate keyword or annotation saying which of the two
      kinds of satisfaction a declaration intends, where the projection and
      the binder already carry it.
      Cost accepted:        a reader must know which of the two projections
      a block wrote before the binders in its body can be read.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-02  What an accessor buys a closed projection
      Argues for:  elem.accessor.1, elem.accessor.limits.1, vis.projection.1
      Source:      rules.txt:236-242

      Beyond writing to an element, an accessor -- a `[]` member projecting
      `ptr<T>` -- is what makes `-> {a, b}` usable on a container. Without
      one, every caller reaches around `[]` into the raw `items` field, so
      closing the projection breaks every program that touches a List. With
      one, `List` exports six names and hides its storage. It does not hide
      POINTERS -- `span` hands out a slice and `[]` hands out a `ptr<T>` --
      but nothing outside can see `items` or `capacity`, which is the whole
      of what the projection ever promised.

      Alternatives weighed: none recorded.
      Cost accepted:        none recorded here; the price of the accessor
      itself is R-34.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-03  Order independence now stands on one leg
      Argues for:  order.independent.1
      Source:      rules.txt:589-594

      Declarations in a block, and blocks in a file, are order-independent,
      because recursive types force it: `Slot` holds an `Entry` declared
      below it. That was one of three reasons and is now the only one --
      the others were the solver needing to have seen every anonymous
      declaration before it could pick, and coherence being a whole-program
      property. Both went out with the instance pool (R-35). A rule left
      standing on one leg is worth noticing; this leg holds.

      Alternatives weighed: none recorded. The two reasons the rule has
      lost are recorded above precisely so that the rule is not later
      re-justified from them.
      Cost accepted:        none recorded.

HOLES AND ABSTRACTION
============================================================================

R-04  A hole is the whole of what an interface ever was
      Argues for:  hole.conjunctive.1, hole.meaning.1, hole.nokeywords.1
      Source:      rules.txt:89-94

      Under `-> self`, holes are read conjunctively: all of them must be
      filled to instantiate, so a block with an unfilled hole cannot be
      instantiated and is usable only as a bound or behind a pointer. That
      is all an interface ever was, and it applies to every block equally
      -- a List with one unfilled field is abstract for the same reason
      Allocator is. Filling the holes gives you a value.

      The consequence is that `Allocator` and `Option` are the same
      declaration shape read two ways, and that no `trait`, `interface`,
      `abstract`, `enum` or `union` is needed to tell them apart.

      [Editor: this argument assumes that an unfilled hole is what makes a
      block abstract, which is what BODILESS MEANS HOLE (rules.txt:132-136)
      then leans on to declare a bodiless header an abstract module. That
      conflicts with a bodiless record -- `Span :: (lo: u32, hi: u32)
      -> self` at rules.txt:81 and json.z:77, which is bodiless and is not
      abstract -- see spec.txt CONFLICT marker.]

      Alternatives weighed: a separate abstraction construct, rejected on
      the ground that the hole reading already covers it for every block
      and not only for blocks that opted in.
      Cost accepted:        none recorded.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-05  Shapes and policies, and why one syntax is enough
      Argues for:  abs.both.1
      Source:      rules.txt:636-637, 671

      Rust conflates shapes and policies and pays for it with orphan rules.
      Go has only shapes. Haskell has only policies. You can have both, told
      apart by one word: whether the holes take `self`.

      Neither is a new construct. Both are a block with unfilled holes.

      Alternatives weighed: Rust's single conflated construct, which buys
      one mechanism and pays in orphan rules; Go's shapes-only and
      Haskell's policies-only, each of which simply lacks the other half.
      Cost accepted:        none recorded.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-06  What a shape is
      Argues for:  abs.shape.1
      Source:      rules.txt:639-643

      SHAPE: every hole takes `self`. An allocator, a writer, a socket --
      things where the abstraction is a property of the object and there is
      exactly one sensible answer per type. Satisfied structurally by any
      type with matching methods, so it needs no registration. Coerce to
      `ptr<Shape>` for dynamic dispatch.

      Alternatives weighed: nominal registration, rejected because
      structural satisfaction needs none.
      Cost accepted:        the adapter problem -- see R-10.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-07  What a policy is
      Argues for:  abs.policy.1, ct.policy.1
      Source:      rules.txt:645-649

      POLICY: no hole takes `self`. `Ord<u8>`, `Formattable<u8>`, `Key<K>`
      -- things where several answers exist and the caller must choose. A
      free-standing NAMED value, passed as a compile-time argument. Works on
      primitives and foreign types, since nothing has to be attached to
      them.

      Alternatives weighed: attaching the choice to the type, which cannot
      be done for a primitive or a foreign type and cannot represent two
      answers at once.
      Cost accepted:        the call site names the instance. See R-35.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-08  The test answers questions it was not designed for
      Argues for:  abs.shape.1, abs.policy.1, op.members.1, op.notoperators.1
      Source:      rules.txt:656-659

      The shape / policy test -- CAN THERE LEGITIMATELY BE TWO OF THESE FOR
      ONE TYPE? -- answers questions it was not designed for, which is the
      best evidence available that it is real rather than tidy. Asked of the
      operators it says `[]` and `()` are members and `+` and `==` are
      policies -- and it is still right, which is exactly why losing the
      solver cost the language arithmetic rather than merely respelling it.
      See R-35.

      [Editor: the operator split at rules.txt:444-447 is itself derived
      from this test, so citing that split back as independent evidence
      that the test is real is circular. The evidence is not worthless --
      the test does produce a determinate answer where the operators are
      concerned -- but it is not evidence from an unrelated question.]

      Alternatives weighed: a stylistic reading of the shape / policy split,
      rejected here as the thing the test is being shown not to be.
      Cost accepted:        none recorded.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-09  Shape or policy is a property of the block
      Argues for:  abs.nomix.1
      Source:      rules.txt:661-663

      The shape / policy distinction is a property of the BLOCK, not of each
      hole. A block with one `self`-taking value hole and one non-`self`
      value hole is an error; it would have no dispatch story and no
      coercion, and admitting it buys nothing. Because every hole is a value
      hole, the test never has to except anything.

      Alternatives weighed: admitting the mixed block as a third kind of
      abstraction, rejected for having neither a dispatch story nor a
      coercion.
      Cost accepted:        none recorded.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-10  Structural matching has no adapter
      Argues for:  abs.shape.1, abs.noadapter.1
      Source:      rules.txt:623-631
      [Fix: this is the argument rules.txt:799 and rules.txt:876 both cite
      as being in NOTE 1. It is not in NOTE 1; it is in the TWO KINDS OF
      ABSTRACTION section. Both pointers now land here.]

      Structural matching has no adapter, and does not get one. A foreign
      type with the right operations under the wrong names cannot be made to
      satisfy a shape except by a wrapper costing a pointer, a vtable and a
      name. Go has the same hole. Every fix for it -- a rename clause, an
      impl block, a coercion declaration -- is nominal registration, which
      is the thing structural matching exists to avoid; and the escape hatch
      is real, since a POLICY may be written for a type you do not own. The
      wrapper is the price of the shape half.

      Alternatives weighed:
        - a rename clause          -- nominal registration
        - an impl block            -- nominal registration
        - a coercion declaration   -- nominal registration
      Cost accepted:        a wrapper per foreign type, costing a pointer,
      a vtable and a name. Where the abstraction can be a policy instead,
      the wrapper is not needed, because a policy attaches to nothing.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-11  A shape carries no provided methods
      Argues for:  abs.shape.holesonly.1 (see RULES TO PROMOTE, 3)
      Source:      rules.txt:698-711

      A shape COULD carry provided methods -- `make :: <T> ...` with a body,
      non-virtual, generic, overridable by an implementer. That buys
      `a.make<T>(n)` and costs three things:

        - `:` vs `::` has to be read carefully inside every shape
        - an implementer can override a provided method, and that override
          silently vanishes the moment the value is coerced to `ptr<Shape>`,
          because non-virtual methods are not in the table
        - the shape's author owns the convenience layer forever

      Keeping them out, as free functions over `M: Allocator`, costs the
      module name at the call site -- `mem.make<T>(allocator, capacity)` and
      not `allocator.make<T>(capacity)`, since there is no UFCS to supply a
      fallback (R-22, R-23). All three problems go for those four
      characters.

      Alternatives weighed: provided methods on the shape, itemised above
      with their three costs.
      Cost accepted:        four characters of module name at every call
      site of a convenience.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-12  What no provided methods costs, and the specialisation given up
      Argues for:  abs.shape.holesonly.1 (see RULES TO PROMOTE, 3)
      Source:      rules.txt:713-722

      What keeping provided methods out of a shape costs: a Pool cannot
      specialize `make` with per-type free lists. If that need becomes real,
      the answer is a separate `TypedAllocator<T>` shape -- monomorphic
      holes per instantiation, so the specialization is a vtable slot and
      survives coercion -- not a default.

      Background, since the tension is real for anything with a table:

          GENERIC . OVERRIDABLE . VIRTUAL  --  pick two.

      Shape-level generics escape it by multiplying vtables, which is why
      `TypedAllocator<T>` is a shape per T and a generic `make` inside
      `Allocator` is not a method at all.

      Alternatives weighed: a defaulted `make` overridable per allocator,
      rejected because the override does not survive coercion to
      `ptr<Shape>`; a shape per element type, accepted as the answer if the
      need becomes real.
      Cost accepted:        no per-type specialisation of a convenience
      until a second shape is written for it.

MEMORY AND POINTERS
============================================================================

R-13  A value does not move
      Argues for:  mem.nomove.1, mem.construct.1, mem.bindaddr.1,
                   mem.interior.1, mem.copy.1
      Source:      rules.txt:263-268

      The rule that a value has one address for as long as it is alive is
      load-bearing, because three things in the language are an address kept
      past the expression that made it: an element pointer handed out by
      `[]`, a closure stored in data, and a `ptr<Shape>` in a container.
      None of them means anything without it. It is also the cheapest rule
      that carries them -- the alternative is move semantics, and move
      semantics is a borrow checker.

      Alternatives weighed: move semantics, rejected because it brings a
      borrow checker with it.
      Cost accepted:        the language cannot relocate a value on its own
      behalf, ever. What it does not promise is R-14.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-14  What one address per value does not promise
      Argues for:  mem.nomove.1, unchecked.txt U.1.1 (was mem.realloc.1)
      Source:      rules.txt:270-276

      "A value does not move" does not promise that a pointer into a
      CONTAINER survives the container's own reallocation. `List.push` may
      allocate a bigger buffer and copy the elements into it. That copy is
      written by hand, in the container's own code, and it is a genuine
      relocation of every element -- the language simply never inserts one
      you did not write. Every pointer into the old buffer is then dangling,
      and nothing catches it. That is the invalidation half of WHO MAY
      WRITE, which is open, and it is about a buffer, not about values.

      Alternatives weighed: none recorded.
      Cost accepted:        a dangling interior pointer after any container
      growth, uncaught.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-15  No automatic destruction
      Argues for:  mem.nodtor.1, unchecked.txt U.2.1 (was mem.leaks.1)
      Source:      rules.txt:278-287

      A block does not get a destructor that runs at scope exit. `deinit` is
      an ordinary member and `defer` is how you call it, which is the same
      idea written where it can be seen.

      The alternative is not one feature: once destructors fire on their
      own, the language must also define what happens on assignment over a
      live value, on return, and on copy, and that is the road to move
      semantics. This language's claim is that cost is visible and there is
      no hidden machinery; a destructor at a closing brace is hidden
      machinery.

      Alternatives weighed: automatic destruction at scope exit, rejected
      because it forces rules for assignment-over-live-value, return and
      copy, which together are move semantics.
      Cost accepted, with open eyes:
        - containers do not free their elements
        - an erased value in a container is kept alive by hand
        - both are leaks the compiler will not mention

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-16  Erasure is a borrow, and there is no Box
      Argues for:  mem.borrow.1, mem.nobox.1,
                   unchecked.txt U.1.3 (was mem.keepalive.1)
      Source:      rules.txt:289-297

      `ptr<Shape>` is a pointer with no owner. That is right for an argument
      and thin for a container: a schedule of `ptr<System>` holds pointers
      into locals, so declaration order in `main` is load-bearing and
      unenforced. The fix would be a `Box<Shape, M>`, which cannot exist
      without a destructor to free it -- so with destruction decided (R-15),
      this is decided with it. YOU KEEP THE VALUES ALIVE YOURSELF: declare
      the concrete values in a scope that outlives the container of
      pointers.

      Alternatives weighed: `Box<Shape, M>`, rejected as unbuildable
      without automatic destruction.
      Cost accepted:        it is a real cost, it is paid in ecs.z's `main`,
      and it is the same trade as R-15.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-17  A parameter is a pointer, written as one
      Argues for:  elem.outparam.1, ptr.arg.explicit.1,
                   unchecked.txt U.1.4 (was elem.ptr.unrestricted.1)
      Source:      rules.txt:211-234

      `&self` is a receiver and stays one; the tempting generalisation -- a
      `(out: &u32)` that is `&self` without the special case -- is not
      taken. Written with a pointer it is C, and it costs one token in one
      body:

          tally :: (out: ptr<u32>, v: Json) -> () { out.* += 1 }
          sort  :: <T, O: Ord<T>> (xs: ptr<List<T, _>>) -> ()

      THE CALL SITE IS IDENTICAL EITHER WAY -- `sort(&xs)`, an argument is
      explicit -- and that is the argument. The place form was invisible to
      every reader of a call and paid only readers of signatures, where it
      promised one thing that a pointer cannot:

          print :: (w: &World) -> ()        I will not keep this
          keep  :: (w: ptr<World>) -> ()    I might store this

      A promise worth having, and not worth what it cost. A pointer is an
      unrestricted handle of indefinite life, and a parameter list is not
      the place to pretend otherwise.

      Nothing is lost in the body either, because `.` and a symbolic member
      both reach through any number of pointers: `xs[j].*` and `xs.count`
      read the same whether `xs` is a List or a `ptr<List>`. `sort`'s body
      did not change when its signature did.

      Alternatives weighed: a `&T` place parameter, generalising `&self`.
      Rejected: identical at the call site, and its one benefit -- a
      signature promising not to retain -- is a promise the pointer model
      cannot back.
      Cost accepted:        a signature can no longer say "I will not keep
      this".

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-18  Deref is never optional
      Argues for:  ptr.derefrequired.1
      Source:      rules.txt:520-523

      Full auto-deref is not on offer, because two acts must not be one
      text:

          p = q         make the two pointers equal
          p.* = q.*     copy the pointee

      Both are things you have to be able to say.

      Alternatives weighed: full auto-deref, rejected because it collapses
      the two spellings above into one.
      Cost accepted:        the explicit `.*` at every whole-pointee read
      and write. The alias hazard it does not close is itemised in R-34.

DISPATCH AND POLICIES
============================================================================

R-19  Static versus dynamic is a type, not a keyword
      Argues for:  abs.bound.1, abs.erased.1, abs.nodyn.1
      Source:      rules.txt:676-682

          List<u8>(mem)      M = Heap            direct calls, 0-byte field
          List<u8>(audited)  M = Tracking<Heap>  direct calls, still mono
          List<u8>(boxed)    M = ptr<Allocator>  fat pointer, indirect calls

      One declaration, three behaviors. The cost of the first two: they are
      different types and cannot share an array. That is the trade, and it
      is visible in the code instead of hidden in a keyword.

      Alternatives weighed: a `dyn` keyword or equivalent annotation,
      rejected because the bracket already separates compile time from run
      time and so separates static from dynamic for the same reason.
      Cost accepted:        two monomorphisations of one declaration are
      different types and cannot share an array.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-20  Monomorphisation is viral, and the alias that stops it
      Argues for:  abs.bound.1
      Source:      rules.txt:684-693

      Monomorphisation is viral through containing types -- a `List<T, M>`
      field makes its owner generic in M, and so on up. Livable, and the
      answer is an alias collapsing the parameter once in the application:
      `A :: ptr<Arena>`, `Doc :: Json<A>`. Two unrelated programs, json.z
      and ecs.z, reached for the same one-line fix, so it is a pattern
      rather than a coincidence.

      The residual cost is ONE ALLOCATOR PER TYPE, and it does not reach a
      library shape: `System<M>` stays generic because a library cannot name
      the application's allocator.

      The trade gets worse for RECURSIVE types, which is open.

      Alternatives weighed: none recorded; the alias is offered as the
      mitigation rather than as an alternative design.
      Cost accepted:
        - one allocator per type, in the application
        - library shapes stay generic and cannot be collapsed
        - recursive types are not answered at all

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-21  Inference from the expected type is checking, not searching
      Argues for:  ct.expected.1, ct.noexpected.1
      Source:      rules.txt:540-550

      A type argument is also inferred from the expected type, which matters
      now that instances are applied by hand. In

          to_string<F = list_fmt<F = fmt_u8>>(heap, xs)

      `list_fmt`'s own `T` and `M` are never written: the position it sits
      in wants a `Formattable<List<u8, Heap>>`, so they are read off that.
      This is CHECKING and not searching -- one expected type, matched
      against one declaration, with nothing to look up and nothing that
      could be ambiguous. Where there is no expected type there is nothing
      to read, and the args are written: `doc_fmt :: json_fmt<ptr<Arena>>`
      in json.z.

      The distinction matters because searching is what R-35 records the
      removal of; this is the part of the convenience that survived, and it
      survived precisely because it is local.

      [Editor: this argument assumes that applying compile-time args to a
      declaration yields a TYPE (rules.txt:32), which is false of every
      policy instance -- `list_fmt<F = fmt_u8>` in the example above is a
      VALUE, as are `json_fmt<ptr<Arena>>`, `spaced<ptr<Arena>>`, `dump<>`
      and `dump<indent = 4>` -- see spec.txt CONFLICT marker.]

      Alternatives weighed: a search over instances, rejected as
      whole-program -- see R-35.
      Cost accepted:        where there is no expected type, the arguments
      are written out.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-22  With no UFCS the split is visible in the text
      Argues for:  call.noufcs.1, call.intrinsic.1, call.extrinsic.1
      Source:      rules.txt:730-738

      Members of a block get T, M and the fields in scope for free, and they
      are the only thing shape matching looks at. That divides operations in
      two:

          INTRINSIC   declared in the block. Counts for shape satisfaction.
                      May be virtual. `push`, `alloc`, `write_bytes`.
          EXTRINSIC   a free function over a bound. Convenience only, never
                      satisfies a shape. `make`, `drop`, `write`.

      With no UFCS the split is visible in the text. `w.write_bytes(p, n)`
      is the shape's and `io.write(w, s)` is not, and the two do not look
      alike, so nobody has to know what a file imported to tell an operation
      from a convenience.

      Alternatives weighed: see R-23.
      Cost accepted:        see R-23.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-23  A method-position fallback is either unsound or invisible
      Argues for:  call.noufcs.1
      Source:      rules.txt:740-752

      The alternative is what makes the no-UFCS rule worth stating. Let
      `a.f(x)` fall back to `f(a, x)` resolved in the CALLER's scope and one
      of two things must be true: either the fallback can satisfy a shape,
      and "does Heap satisfy Allocator" becomes a question about imports --
      two modules coerce the same value and build different vtables -- or it
      cannot, and the language has a method call whose meaning depends on a
      distinction the call site hides. The first is unsound and the second
      is the reader's problem forever. Both are avoided by the same one-line
      rule.

      You still get extension functions -- write anything over a type you do
      not own -- they just cannot join a shape, and they cannot dress as
      members.

      Alternatives weighed:
        - a fallback that CAN satisfy a shape   -- unsound: satisfaction
          becomes a property of the importer, and two modules build
          different vtables for one value
        - a fallback that CANNOT satisfy a shape -- sound, but the call site
          hides which kind of call it is
      Cost accepted:        `mem.make<T>(allocator, n)` where
      `allocator.make<T>(n)` would have read more like prose.

R-24  The name `oneof`, and why it is spelled bare
      Argues for:  decl.proj.oneof.1
      Source:      rules.txt:138-144

      `-> T` is already glossed "one value escapes", so `-> one` would
      collide with the projection above it, and a bare `one` in a slot that
      otherwise holds type names burns a plausible identifier. `union` is
      poisoned by C, `variant` names a member rather than the whole,
      `either` implies two.

      It is spelled bare and not `-> oneof self` because a sum does not
      project the whole block -- one member escapes, which puts it in
      `{a, b}`'s family.

      Alternatives weighed:
        - `one`      -- collides with `-> T`, and burns an identifier
        - `union`    -- poisoned by C
        - `variant`  -- names a member, not the whole
        - `either`   -- implies two
        - `oneof self` -- wrong family; a sum projects one member, not the
          block
      Cost accepted:        none recorded.

R-25  `.*` is the shape of the rule, not decoration
      Argues for:  ptr.deref.1, ptr.reach.dot.1
      Source:      rules.txt:502-505

      `.` reaches through and stops at a member; `.*` reaches through and
      stops at the value. The two spellings are the same act ending in
      different places, which is why the deref lives after the dot rather
      than being its own symbol.

      Alternatives weighed: a prefix or standalone deref symbol, rejected
      because it would not show that the deref and the member selection are
      the same act.
      Cost accepted:        none recorded.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-26  `^` is free for xor, so deref is the half that moved
      Argues for:  ptr.deref.1
      Source:      rules.txt:515-518

      A hash function writes `h = (h ^ k[i]) * prime`, and a language whose
      deref is also postfix `^` eventually has to parse `h^ ^ x`. Bitwise
      and (`&`) has the same problem against address-of; deref was the half
      that could move.

      `.*` is available BECAUSE `*` is not a member name. `[]` and `()` are,
      so `f.()(x)` and `a.[](i)` are real spellings; had arithmetic stayed a
      member this deref would have collided with the multiply member on the
      first day.

      Alternatives weighed: postfix `^` as deref, rejected on the parse
      of `h^ ^ x`; `&` as deref, unavailable against address-of.
      Cost accepted:        none recorded.

----------------------------------------------------------------------------
R-27  Naming the member `[]` spends no keyword
      Argues for:  op.nokeyword.1, op.members.1
      Source:      rules.txt:757-759

      `operator []` would be the language's only two-token name, and the
      only place the word `operator` appears. Naming the member `[]` costs
      nothing and spends no keyword.

      Alternatives weighed: an `operator` keyword, rejected as a two-token
      name introduced for one purpose.
      Cost accepted:        none recorded.

R-28  A closure is a block with a `()` member
      Argues for:  op.closure.1, op.closure.capture.1, op.nocurry.1
      Source:      rules.txt:481-484, 809-824

      A closure is not a special form. It is a block with a `()` member,
      which is why `Fn<A, R>` is an ordinary shape and needs no
      function-type machinery, and why there is no curried spelling: what a
      closure captures, it captures as a field, in a block written out like
      any other.

      The member half of the operator decision pays for itself twice,
      because the same move that names a member `[]` names a member `()`,
      and that is what a closure is:

          print_u8 :: <M: Allocator> (allocator: M) -> self {
              () :: (&self, v: u8) -> () {
                  io.write(io.out, to_string(self.allocator, v))
              }
          }

      -- an ordinary `-> self` block, whose runtime params are its captured
      fields, exactly as everywhere else. There is no curried spelling and
      no second runtime group: the declaration form has one, and a closure
      needs nothing beyond a block. Two questions answer themselves:
      `print_u8(mem)` has its OWN type (so it is free), and it becomes a
      shared type precisely when you write `ptr<Fn<u8, ()>>`, which is the
      same coercion as `ptr<Allocator>` and not a second mechanism.

      Alternatives weighed: a function type and a curried spelling, neither
      needed once `()` is an ordinary member name.
      Cost accepted:        none recorded.

R-29  Precedence is a fixed table
      Argues for:  op.precedence.1
      Source:      rules.txt:474-475, 806-807

      Precedence is a fixed table and is not user-extensible, which now
      costs nothing to say, because nothing is extensible. It always was a
      fixed table, and it is now merely a fact about the grammar rather than
      a cost, since there is nothing to extend.

      [Editor: the original states this twice, at rules.txt:474-475 and
      again at rules.txt:806-807, in near-identical words. Both statements
      are folded into this one entry.]

      Alternatives weighed: user-extensible precedence, which has no
      standing once operators are not overloadable at all (R-35).
      Cost accepted:        none recorded; the original explicitly records
      that this stopped being a cost when the solver went.

R-30  A match is an expression
      Argues for:  match.expr.1
      Source:      rules.txt:385-395

      Match being an expression is what keeps sum types from turning code
      into a pyramid. A one-arm-plus-`_` match BINDS instead of nesting:

          obj  := match (doc)         { object (o) { o }  _ { return } }
          tags := match (obj["tags"]) { some   (t) { t }  _ { return } }
          xs   := match (tags)        { array  (a) { a }  _ { return } }

      Each line either produces a value or leaves. Nested matches are for
      when the failure arms do real work; when they do not, bind and move
      on. Languages that miss this end up adding `if let`, `guard let` and
      `let else` as three separate forms.

      Alternatives weighed: statement-only match plus dedicated binding
      forms, rejected as three forms where one expression does the work.
      Cost accepted:        none recorded.

R-31  What exhaustiveness costs, and why `_` is chosen rather than defaulted
      Argues for:  match.exhaustive.1, match.growth.1
      Source:      rules.txt:368-383

      A match names every variant, or ends in `_`. It is checked, and the
      check is the only thing making the one-arm binding form (R-30) safe to
      write: `_` is what the arms you did not name collapse into, so leaving
      it off is how you ask to be told when a sum grows a variant.

      Two costs, both already paid in the corpus and neither of them a
      surprise:

        - a match that CANNOT reach an arm must still write it. json.z's
          `get` knows the slot is `live` because `probe` said `found`, and
          still spells `empty` and `dead`. Every container grows a tail of
          dead arms.
        - a TAG TEST -- "is this the `dead` variant" -- has no payload to
          bind and no short spelling, so it is a full match too. The
          discriminant is not a value, which is open, and that is the same
          gap read from the other side.

      Writing `_` silences both, and silences the growth warning with them.
      That is the trade, and it is the reason `_` is a spelling you choose
      rather than the default.

      [Editor: the tag-test cost was subsequently worked around rather than
      paid -- json.z:383 writes `if (tag_of(t) != tag_of(want))` and
      json.z:828 admits `tag_of` is invented. The cost as stated is
      therefore understated by the corpus that was supposed to demonstrate
      it.]

      Alternatives weighed: `_` as the default, rejected because it would
      silence the growth diagnostic for every match that did not opt out.
      Cost accepted:
        - a tail of unreachable arms in every container
        - a tag test spelled as a full match

R-32  A value may not be dropped silently
      Argues for:  wild.nodrop.1, wild.discard.1
      Source:      rules.txt:423-432

          xs.push(v)              fine -- push projects `()`, no value
          xs.pop()                an error: an Option<T> went nowhere
          _ := xs.pop()           "bind it to nothing", said out loud

      The rule exists because an ignored error is the one mistake a language
      must not make easy to write, and with no `try` (R-36) the error
      channel is an ordinary returned value like any other -- which is
      exactly why nothing but this rule is watching it.

      Alternatives weighed: none recorded.
      Cost accepted:        the explicit `_ :=` at every deliberate discard.

R-33  `_` does not reach projection position
      Argues for:  wild.noprojection.1, wild.independent.1
      Source:      rules.txt:434-441

      `_` fixes the CONSUMERS of a plumbing parameter and not the WRAPPERS:
      a function that RETURNS a `List<T, M>` must name the M it declined to
      name in its parameters, because a projection is a type and `_` is the
      absence of one. Two `_` are also two independent parameters, so `_`
      can never say "the same type I did not name". Both gaps would want a
      real existential, which is a larger feature than the convenience is
      worth.

      This is a cost the language accepts rather than a gap it intends to
      close.

      Alternatives weighed: an existential type, rejected as larger than the
      convenience it would buy.
      Cost accepted:
        - a wrapper must name the plumbing parameter its callees decline
        - `_` cannot refer back to an earlier `_`

R-34  CUT: the `-> &T` place projection
      Argues for:  elem.noplace.1, elem.accessor.1, elem.noamptype.1,
                   elem.rawindex.1, elem.polyindex.1, elem.fallible.1,
                   elem.noproxy.1
      Source:      rules.txt:165-209; reversal recorded as [P22] in ecs.z
                   (ecs.z:1090-1130)

      An earlier design had a fourth projection -- `-> &T`, "a LOCATION
      escapes" -- so that `xs[i] = v` could be written bare. It is cut, and
      the argument that killed it is the one made against place PARAMETERS
      (R-17), re-run and decided the same way: the call site is what a
      reader reads, and `.*` there says a pointer is being written through,
      in a language whose whole claim is that memory is the programmer's
      business. `sort(&xs)` spells its address-of for the same reason.

      What replaced it is the one pointer type the language already has:

          [] :: (&self, i: u32) -> ptr<T> { &self.items[i] }

          v := xs[i].*      read it
          xs[i].* = v       write it
          xs[i].* += 1      both, and `[]` is called exactly once
          p := xs[i]        keep its address, already a ptr<T>

      KEEPING IT COST FOUR RULES, and the first had to reach into every type
      position in the language to forbid `&T` fields, `&T` arguments,
      `Option<&V>` and `List<&T>` -- so that `&` could not quietly become a
      second pointer type. With the projection gone, `&` stops being a type
      constructor: those four spellings are ungrammatical rather than
      prohibited, and the rule forbidding them is not needed. Nothing below
      the syntax changed either -- `-> &T` was `ptr<T>` underneath, with the
      compiler inserting the deref the source now writes.

      What decided it was counting the feature's use against its
      specification: one declaration in the whole corpus -- `List` in
      unified.z -- against four rules.

      A FALLIBLE LOOKUP HANDS BACK A VALUE, and WHICH value is the
      container's choice and not the language's: `Option<V>` is a copy,
      `Option<ptr<V>>` points into the container's own storage so the match
      arm can write through it. A packed container -- a bitset, a compressed
      array, an element computed on the fly -- has no address to hand back
      at all, and offers `get(i)` and `set(i, b)`. THERE ARE NO PROXY
      OBJECTS, which is the disease `vector<bool>` died of.

      [Editor: this argument assumes `[]` has a determinate meaning at each
      use, which it does not -- `[]` carries at least four: the element's
      address on a List, an `Option<V>` on a Map, the element itself on a
      raw pointer, and a reach-through on a `ptr<Container>` -- see spec.txt
      CONFLICT marker.]

      [Editor: this argument also asserts that `&` stops being a type
      constructor entirely, which is false of `&self` -- see spec.txt
      CONFLICT marker.]

      Alternatives weighed:
        - `-> &T`, the place projection. Costs four rules, the first of
          which reaches into every type position in the language.
        - a second member `[]=`, which is C++'s split and reintroduces the
          paired-name problem the single-member design removed.
        - `-> ptr<T>` with an explicit deref at the use site. Adopted.

      Cost accepted, itemised, because it is real and is paid at every use:

        - `v := xs[i]` BINDS A POINTER WHERE A COPY WAS MEANT, and compiles.
          The shuffle in insertion sort is the live example: miss the `.*`
          and `v` aliases the array it is about to overwrite, which is a
          wrong answer rather than a crash. Arguments do not auto-deref, so
          passing it on is a type error and catches most of these -- but `.`
          and a symbolic member reach through any number of pointers, so a
          `v.x` read never errors. This is the sharp edge of the cut and it
          does not go away.

        - `p[i]` ON A RAW POINTER IS THE ELEMENT; `xs[i]` ON A LIST IS ITS
          ADDRESS. One syntax, two meanings, and both appear inside List
          itself -- `new_items[i] = self.items[i]` in the grow path,
          `xs[i].* = v` in every caller. Places made these uniform. Nothing
          else does.

        - `[]` HAS NO ONE MEANING ACROSS CONTAINERS. List projects `ptr<T>`,
          Map projects `Option<V>`. That was already true before the cut --
          a fallible lookup was never a place either -- and the cut makes it
          the common case rather than the exception.

R-35  CUT: the instance pool and the solver, and with them operator
      overloading
      Argues for:  ct.nopool.1, ct.named.decl.1, ct.typenotvalue.1,
                   op.notoperators.1, op.instructions.1, op.usertype.1,
                   op.nokeyword.1
      Source:      rules.txt:444-472, 558-586, 762-807
      [Fix: rules.txt:799 sends the reader to "the wrapper problem in
      NOTE 1's last paragraph" for what a numeric shape costs. That
      argument is not in NOTE 1; it is R-10.]

      EVERY DECLARATION HAS A NAME. `::` with nothing on its left is not a
      spelling. There is no anonymous instance, no pool, no search and no
      default: a policy parameter is supplied at the call site, or the call
      does not compile.

      WHAT THIS COST, since it is the largest thing traded away in the
      language. A search would have let `sort(&xs)`, `to_string(heap, v)`
      and `a + b` each find their instance with nothing written. It bought
      that with a global pool, whole-program uniqueness, overlap checking,
      an orphan convention, derived instances resolved by unification, and a
      rule letting an anonymous instance name itself because it had none.
      Every one of those is a whole-program property -- a thing you cannot
      answer by reading the file in front of you. The call site is longer
      now, and it is local.

      OPERATOR OVERLOADING WENT WITH IT, and the reason is worth stating
      exactly, because the policy reading was not wrong.

      Start from where arithmetic cannot go. Written as a member, it breaks
      on the second line of vector code:

          v * 2.0      ->  a member of Vec2 named `*`.  Fine.
          2.0 * v      ->  a member of f32 named `*`.   Nobody owns f32.

      Nothing rescues that. The only candidate is a free function reached
      from method position, which is the fallback R-23 rules out, correctly:
      resolved in the caller's scope, "is Vec2 numeric" would depend on what
      happened to be imported. So a member cannot express scaling, and no
      bound could ever require arithmetic of a type parameter.

      SO THE LANGUAGE HAD IT AS A POLICY. The shape / policy test gives the
      reason: two ways to add two vectors -- saturating and wrapping -- so
      addition is a CHOICE, and a choice is a policy. `a + b` was
      `Add<A, B>.add(a, b)` with the operand types as the search key.
      Everything awkward about the member reading went at once. Scalars on
      the left worked, because a policy is attached to nothing. And
      `lerp :: <T> (a: T, b: T, t: f32) -> T` -- generic code over anything
      numeric -- was sayable, which under the member reading it is not, in
      any form.

      IT DID NOT SURVIVE THE POOL. An operator is a spelling with no room to
      name anything: `a + b` has nowhere to put `Add<Vec2, Vec2>`. So an
      operator can only dispatch to a policy if the policy can be found
      WITHOUT being named, and that is a solver, and the solver is what
      went. What is left once instances must be named is
      `vec2_add.add(a, b)` -- which is a function call with a symbol in
      front of it, not an operator -- or `v.add(w)`, which is at least
      honest about being a method.

      The short of it: the shape / policy test is right, arithmetic IS a
      choice, and a choice wants a policy -- but a policy is only an
      OPERATOR if the caller can leave it unsaid, and leaving it unsaid is a
      solver. `2.0 * v` cannot be written without one, and no member could
      ever have expressed it. THERE IS NO THIRD DESIGN: there is a language
      with a solver, and a language with `v.scale(2.0)`.

      What is left in the language is arithmetic as instructions on the
      primitives, desugaring to nothing, and a user type publishing ordinary
      members -- `v.add(w)`, `v.scale(2.0)`, `a.eq(b)` -- where the call
      site says whose arithmetic it asked for. This is the one place the
      language got worse.

      Alternatives weighed:
        - arithmetic as a member of the left operand -- cannot express
          `2.0 * v`; nobody owns f32
        - a free function reached from method position -- makes "is Vec2
          numeric" depend on the caller's imports (R-23)
        - arithmetic as a policy WITH a solver -- works completely, and
          costs the pool: whole-program uniqueness, overlap checking, an
          orphan convention, and unification
        - arithmetic as a policy WITHOUT a solver -- `vec2_add.add(a, b)`,
          which is not an operator, only a call with a symbol in front

      Cost accepted, itemised, because it is the largest regression in the
      language and should not be quietly reframed as a simplification:
        - `2.0 * v` has no spelling at all. `v.scale(2.0)` is the operation,
          in the other order, from the type that owns it.
        - `lerp` over anything numeric is gone. A generic body cannot say
          `a + b`, so numeric generic code needs a shape with `add`/`scale`
          members and gets the wrapper problem in R-10.
        - the operand types no longer have to agree with anything, so a type
          with `add` and a type with `plus` are simply different.

      What was gained is the whole of the entry above: no pool, no
      coherence, no orphan convention, no unification, and nothing about a
      call that a reader has to leave the file to answer.

R-36  CUT: the `try` keyword
      Argues for:  match.notry.1, match.errchannel.1
      Source:      rules.txt:397-413

      Propagating a failure is an ordinary one-arm match, written out:

          t := match (self.bump()) { ok (t) { t }  err (e) { return err(e) } }

      THERE WAS A `try` KEYWORD and it is cut, because it was the one place
      the language reached into a library type by name. `Result` is declared
      in std.core as an ordinary `-> oneof { ok: T, err: E }` -- no more
      privileged than `Option` or anything you write -- and `try` had to
      know that the second variant is spelled `err`. Asked of a user's
      `-> oneof { a: T, b: E }` it had no answer, and none was ever written.
      Either `Result` is built in, with the standing of `u32` and `ptr`, or
      the keyword goes; the keyword went, and the propagation is spelled at
      all fourteen sites in json.z.

      Alternatives weighed:
        - `Result` built in, with the standing of `u32` and `ptr`. Rejected;
          the keyword went instead.
        - a `try` that works on any two-variant sum. No answer was ever
          written for which variant is the failure.
      Cost accepted:        a line where a word used to be, and it is not
      free: the error arm is the same eight tokens every time, and json.z is
      where you can see whether that reads as honest or as noise.

R-37  CUT: the fallible projection `-> Result<self, E>`
      Argues for:  bind.which.named.1, open.txt OQ-1 (was open.ctorbody.1)
      Source:      rules.txt:840-854

      A `-> self` block has field initialisers and no statements, so
      anything needing `self`, or two fields at once, or a step that can
      fail, has nowhere to go. What is missing is a STATEMENT POSITION, and
      `self` inside it; nothing less answers any of the three.

      A fallible PROJECTION was tried -- `-> Result<self, E>`, so that a
      field initialiser could fail -- and is cut. IT WORKED, AS FAR AS IT
      WENT: the err arm had somewhere to return to when the projection was a
      Result. But it answers only the case where the failure is attached to
      binding a field, it never said which other wraps were legal, and
      `-> SomeType` is already spoken for by the binding rules, where it
      means "fill the holes with `=`" -- which a body full of `:=` is not.
      It belongs to whatever gives the constructor a body, not beside it.

      A value having one address (R-13) is what makes such a body definable
      at all, so this is the next thing to decide.

      Alternatives weighed:
        - `-> Result<self, E>` as a projection. Cut: partial, undefined for
          other wraps, and collides with the meaning `-> SomeType` already
          has.
        - a statement position in a `-> self` body, with `self` available
          inside it. Not designed; recorded as the thing that must come
          first.
      Cost accepted:        two-phase construction, unchecked -- ecs.z's

R-38  CUT: `Iterator`

        - a `for (x := it)` form desugaring to that consumer. Wanted, not

R-39  CUT: associated types, and the `Out` hole
      Argues for:  hole.noassoc.1, hole.valueonly.1, hole.typeparam.1
      Source:      rules.txt:118-122

      A HOLE IS A VALUE, ALWAYS. There is no hole of type `type`, and so
      nothing here of what the rest of the world calls an associated type.
      The one that existed was `Out` in the arithmetic policies -- the
      result type an `Add<A, B>` instance decided for itself -- and it went
      out with them (R-35). A type a block needs is a compile-time
      PARAMETER, which the caller spells.

      The rule pays for itself elsewhere: because every hole is a value
      hole, the shape / policy test never has to except anything (R-09).

      Alternatives weighed: a hole of type `type`, which is what an
      associated type is. It had exactly one use -- `Out` -- and that use
      was removed for an unrelated reason, leaving the feature with none.
      Cost accepted:        a type a block needs must be spelled by the
      caller as a compile-time parameter, rather than chosen by the
      instance.
